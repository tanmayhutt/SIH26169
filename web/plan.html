Teaching a Camera to Find and Follow a Laser Beacon

Official title: Development of an AI-Based Virtual Camera Tracking System for Coarse Alignment of Mobile Free Space Optical Communication (FSOC) Terminals. In plain words: two machines far apart want to talk with a laser beam. Before any data can flow they must find each other and keep looking at each other. This software does that first step, entirely in simulation, with no camera, motor or lens needed.

01

What exists today, and what goes wrong

Everything in this section comes from the background text of the problem statement, put into everyday words.

What is used today

Satellites, drones and ground stations mostly talk over radio today. The problem statement calls these "conventional radio-frequency systems". Radio works, but compared with light it is slow, it needs a licensed slice of the radio spectrum, and it can be upset by electrical interference.

FSOC (Free Space Optical Communication) sends the data on a laser beam instead. The problem statement lists its advantages: data rates from gigabits up to terabits per second, no spectrum licence needed, and high immunity to electromagnetic interference. That is why ISRO wants it for next-generation mobile networks.

Why it is hard

A radio signal spreads out in a wide cone, so a slightly wrong aim still works. A laser beam is, in the problem statement's words, "highly directional". It is a thin pencil of light. "Even a small angular error can prevent successful communication."

And the two ends are moving. Satellites orbit. UAVs fly and shake. So both terminals must keep finding and following each other while everything moves. That job is called PAT: Pointing, Acquisition and Tracking.

PAT has two stages. Coarse alignment gets the far terminal inside the camera's field of view. Fine alignment then locks the beam onto it precisely. This project is the coarse stage. The problem statement breaks it into four steps, and our software follows exactly those four:

  1. Observe the surrounding environment.
  2. Acquire and detect the remote terminal or beacon.
  3. Estimate its position.
  4. Continuously adjust the pointing direction to keep it in view.

The practical problem this project solves

The problem statement says it directly: "Developing and testing such algorithms on real hardware requires expensive cameras, pan-tilt mechanisms, and optical components and equipment." So it asks for "a software based virtual camera tracking" platform that is "inexpensive and accessible" for developing algorithms and for learning.

  • From the problem statement

    The hardware is expensive

    A real camera, a motorised pan and tilt mount and optical parts cost a lot and need a lab. That blocks students, small teams and anyone who wants to learn or try an idea quickly.

  • Our reasoning

    Three more things a simulator fixes

    You can switch on fog, rain or vibration at will instead of waiting for them. You know exactly where the beacon really is, so you can measure error precisely. And you can repeat the same test exactly, so you can tell whether a code change actually helped.

Words you will see on this page

Beacon
The bright spot of light from the far terminal. The thing we must find and follow.
Screen
The whole simulated scene, at least 2000 by 2000 pixels. The beacon moves around on it.
Camera window
The 640 by 480 pixel rectangle that shows where the laser terminal is pointing. It covers 4 by 3 degrees. The job is to keep the beacon inside it, near the middle.
Pan and tilt
Turning the camera window left and right, and up and down, at a limited speed of 5 to 10 degrees per second.
Centre point
The exact middle of the beacon spot, worked out to a fraction of a pixel. The rules call this the centroid.
Lock
The state where the software has found the beacon and is holding it inside the camera window.
02

What goes in, and what comes out

This is the part that is easiest to get wrong, so here it is as plainly as possible. The tracker looks at one thing, controls one thing, and produces one set of outputs.

What the tracker looks at, and what it controls

Looks at

  • The whole scene picture, the screen. At least 2000 by 2000 pixels, black and white, 30 pictures a second.
  • The picture already has the beacon in it, plus whatever disturbances are switched on: fog, shaking, noise.
  • The problem statement calls this the "simulated video stream" and the "virtual camera feed".
  • The tracker never gets told where the beacon really is. It only gets the picture.

Controls

  • The camera window. A 640 by 480 rectangle that stands for where the laser terminal is pointing.
  • It starts in the centre of the screen.
  • It can move at most 5 to 10 degrees per second, like a real motorised mount. It cannot jump.
  • The goal is to get the beacon inside this window fast, and keep it near the middle.

If the graders give us a video, why build our own scene at all

Because the video is one test at the very end. Generating the scene is the product itself.

  • Rule

    It is a mandatory requirement

    The "software shall be able to" list starts with "Generate a configurable virtual environment", "Generate one or more moving targets" and "Implement a movable virtual camera". Parameter rows 1 to 15 exist only to set up that generator. The 20 percent live demo is the graders watching it work.

  • Rule

    Benchmark 1 cannot run without it

    Benchmark 1 gives us scenarios, which are settings, not footage. Our own simulator has to run them, log the centroiding error and write the report. That is 30 percent of the marks that need the generator.

  • Practical

    We will not have their video while building

    Their .mp4 files arrive at judging. For the months before that, our own generator is the only source of test footage, in every weather and every motion pattern, as much as we want.

  • Practical

    Only our own scene knows the true answer

    To know whether the tracker is within 10 pixels, we must know where the beacon really is in each frame. In our own frames we know it exactly. That is how we measure error, catch mistakes when code changes, and train the AI with perfectly labelled pictures.

The split in one line. Their video proves our tracker works on footage we did not make. Our generator is where the tracker is built, tested, trained and demonstrated, and it is where half the marks are awarded directly.

The problem statement lists what the automatic performance report must have. We produce every item, with a one line definition printed next to each number so nobody has to guess what was measured.

Two things worth being clear about. First, the tracker never peeks at the answer. The simulator knows the true beacon position, but uses it only to draw the picture and to mark our score afterwards. Second, in Benchmark 2 our camera window moves over the graders' video, but nothing in the video changes because of it. The marks there are for how well we find and follow the beacon in their footage.
03

Our solution, in plain words

The product has two halves. A simulator that stands in for the whole lab: scene, beacon, camera, motor and disturbances. And a tracker that finds and follows the beacon and steers the camera. Both run on an ordinary laptop with no special hardware.

What we are building

  1. A simulated sky. A 2000 by 2000 picture (or larger) that acts as the world, with stars or terrain in the background.
  2. A simulated beacon. A bright moving spot that can travel in a straight line, a circle, a figure of eight, at random, or in a spiral or wave. You can add more than one.
  3. A camera window. The 640 by 480 rectangle that shows where the laser terminal points. It starts in the middle.
  4. A simulated motor that moves the window. It obeys the same speed limit as a real motor, so the software cannot cheat by jumping.
  5. A disturbance generator. It dirties the picture the way the real world would: fog, rain, low light, air turbulence, platform movement, vibration and sensor noise.
  6. The tracker. It looks at each picture, finds the beacon, measures its centre exactly, predicts where it is heading, and tells the motor which way to turn.
  7. A scoreboard. It measures everything while the run goes on and writes the full report automatically when it ends.

What this changes

Before, with real hardware

  • Needs a camera, a motor mount and optics. Expensive.
  • Wait for fog or wind to test bad conditions.
  • No way to know the beacon's true position, so accuracy cannot be measured.
  • Every run is slightly different, so results cannot be compared.
  • One lab, one team at a time.

After, with this software

  • Runs on any laptop. Cost is zero.
  • Switch fog on with a slider, any time.
  • The simulator drew the beacon, so it knows the exact true position and can measure error precisely.
  • Same settings and same seed give the exact same run, every time.
  • Run hundreds of tests overnight, unattended.
The point worth repeating. Because the software draws the beacon itself, it always knows the correct answer. That is what lets us measure real accuracy, prove the system works, and train the AI. Real hardware can never give you that.
04

Where AI comes in

AI is used at the exact point where the simple method breaks down, and nowhere else. Here is what each method does. Numbers marked as targets are what we are designing for, not results yet.

  1. STEP 1 The simple method, which works most of the time

    The beacon is a bright spot on a darker background. So look for bright pixels, group the ones that touch, and take their weighted average to get the centre. This is standard, well understood image processing.

    It is very fast, and in clear conditions it can measure the centre to a fraction of a pixel. Target: under 3 milliseconds per frame on the full 2000 by 2000 picture.

    Verdict: good enough on a clear day. We always run it first.

  2. STEP 2 Where the simple method fails

    In fog, rain or low light, the beacon becomes faint and the background brighter. "Find the brightest thing" now returns the wrong answer. Sensor noise makes fake bright specks, and salt and pepper noise at 10 percent of the picture makes thousands of them. If several bright objects are in view, brightness alone cannot say which one is ours.

    Verdict: exactly the conditions the rules ask us to survive.

  3. STEP 3 The AI model takes over

    A small neural network looks at the picture and produces a heat map: how likely each spot is to be the real beacon. It has learned what a beacon looks like, its shape, its soft edges, how it sits in noise. So it still finds the spot when the spot is faint.

    The model is kept deliberately small so it runs on a normal laptop processor with no graphics card. Once the beacon is locked, the model only looks at the area around where the beacon is expected, which keeps it fast. Target: under 5 milliseconds per frame.

    Verdict: this is the part that rescues the hard cases.

  4. STEP 4 Where the training data comes from

    Training an AI normally means collecting thousands of pictures and paying people to label each one by hand. We do not have to. Our own simulator draws the pictures, so it already knows exactly where the beacon is in each one.

    That gives unlimited, perfectly labelled training pictures for free, in every weather we can generate. The simulator is both the test bench and the data factory.

    Verdict: no dataset to buy, no hand labelling, no labelling mistakes.

  5. STEP 5 Knowing which target is yours

    The rules allow more than one beacon. The software must keep following the one it was told to follow, even when two cross paths. It does this by combining how each target looks with how each has been moving. The one that matches the expected path is ours.

    Verdict: identity is held through crossings, not guessed again each frame.

  6. STEP 6 Predicting the next position

    The tracker keeps a model of how the beacon is moving. It runs several motion models at once, moving straight, speeding up, and turning, and trusts whichever one is matching reality. This covers every path type in the rules without retuning.

    So if the beacon vanishes into noise for a moment, the camera window does not freeze or wander. It keeps moving to where the beacon should be, and picks it up again quickly. This is how we aim to meet the 1 second re-acquisition limit.

    Verdict: prediction turns a brief loss into a non event.

The safety rule we never break. The AI only runs when the simple method is struggling. The simple, fast method is always there underneath as a fallback. So the system cannot become slow or unreliable just because the model had a bad day.

The numbers behind the design

None of these appear in the rules directly. They come from simple arithmetic on the numbers that do. One assumption is needed, and it is stated first.

The one assumption. We treat one pixel of the screen as one pixel of the camera window. So the window is a 640 by 480 cut-out of the screen, and since 640 pixels span 4 degrees, the whole 2000 pixel screen spans 12.5 degrees. This is a setting and can be changed.

At the maximum platform motion setting, three quarters of the motor's speed goes on cancelling the drift, and only a quarter is left for actually following the beacon. This is why the controller must predict, not just react.

What this means for finding the beacon

Because the tracker looks at the whole screen, spotting the beacon takes only a few frames. Turning the window from the centre to the farthest corner then takes about 1 second at 5 degrees per second, with both axes moving together. Add a few frames to confirm the detection is real and the total sits inside the 2 second limit from any starting point.

There is also a hard mode setting, where the tracker is allowed to see only the inside of the camera window, as a real narrow camera would. Then it has to sweep the screen in a spiral. The screen holds about 13 window sized areas, so a beacon hidden in a far corner takes several seconds to reach, and the log records that honestly. This mode is for testing the algorithm under harder conditions, not the default.

  • Design rule 1

    The window cannot jump

    It is tempting to just place the window on top of the beacon every frame. That is not allowed. The motor has a speed limit, and the drift is eating most of it. So the controller works out a turn rate every frame, never above the limit, and uses the predicted position to lead the target rather than chase it.

  • Design rule 2

    Log both errors, with clear names

    The rules talk about tracking error in the specifications and centroiding error in the benchmark rounds. We measure both every frame. Tracking error: how far the true beacon sits from the middle of the window. Centroiding error: how far our measured centre is from the true centre. Whichever a grader means, the number is there with its definition.

  • Design rule 3

    Do not chase the vibration

    Vibration jerks the picture by up to 20 pixels every frame in a random direction. A motor cannot follow that and should not try. The tracker smooths it out and the controller follows the smoothed position. It also measures how much the whole picture shifted between frames and cancels that drift directly.

How the software is built

Six layers. Three of them do the real work and pass information round in a loop: the World draws the picture, Perception finds the beacon, and Control moves the window.

The layers

DISPLAY         What you see: the screen with the window drawn on it, live graphs, settings
      ^
SCOREBOARD      Collects every measurement, writes the CSV log and the PDF report
      ^
CLOCK           Runs everything at a steady 30 pictures per second, same seed = same run
      |                       |                          |
WORLD                 PERCEPTION                  CONTROL
the sky                where is the beacon         which way to turn
the beacon(s)          simple detector             decide the state
the camera window      AI detector                 work out the error
the motor              exact centre point          add the prediction
disturbances           which one is ours           respect the speed limit
draw the picture       predict where it goes       send the command
      ^______________ turn command goes back to the motor ______|

What happens in one frame, thirty times a second

  1. The beacon moves a little. The platform drifts and shakes a little.
  2. The motor moves the camera window by however much it was told last frame.
  3. The software draws the scene picture, then dirties it with fog, turbulence, drift, shake and sensor noise. In Benchmark 2 this step is skipped and a video frame is used instead.
  4. The picture goes to the simple detector. If the detector is unsure, the AI model is asked as well.
  5. The exact centre of the beacon is measured, to a fraction of a pixel.
  6. The predictor updates its idea of where the beacon is going.
  7. The software decides its state: searching, checking, following, coasting, or re-finding.
  8. It works out how far the beacon is from the middle of the window and turns that into a motor command, capped at the motor's speed limit.
  9. Every number from this frame is written to the log and drawn on the live graphs.

The five states the tracker moves through

StateWhat it meansWhat the window does
SEARCHNo beacon found yetLooks at the whole picture for it. In hard mode, sweeps outward in a spiral.
VERIFYFound something, but is it realWaits for 3 good frames out of 4 before trusting it, so a noise speck is not mistaken for the beacon
TRACKLocked on and followingMoves so the beacon stays in the middle
COASTLost sight of it for a momentKeeps moving to where the prediction says it should be
REACQUIREStill missing, go and find itLooks again around the predicted spot, widening as the uncertainty grows

What each part does

PartIts job, in plain words
Scene makerDraws the background: a star field, ground terrain, or plain sky. You set the screen size, the window size and its angle of view. Can add other bright objects to test the tracker on purpose.
Beacon makerCreates one or more moving spots. You pick the shape, the size from 5 to 20 pixels, the start point and the path: straight, circular, figure of eight, random, spiral or wave.
Window and motorKeeps track of where the camera window is pointing. The motor has a top speed, a top acceleration and a small delay, like a real one.
Disturbance generatorAdds fog, rain, haze and low light, plus air turbulence, platform drift, vibration and sensor noise. Each one can be switched on or off on its own and set to any strength up to the limits in the rules.
DetectorFinds the beacon in the picture. Runs the fast simple method first, then the AI model when the picture is hard. Tells several targets apart.
PredictorKeeps track of how the beacon is moving and estimates where it will be next. Runs several motion models at once and trusts whichever is matching reality.
ControllerTurns "the beacon is 40 pixels left of the middle" into "move left at 3 degrees per second", never over the speed limit, never over correcting, and leading the target using the prediction.
ScoreboardRecords about 30 numbers every frame, then writes a CSV file and a PDF report with graphs, pass or fail against each rule, and the settings used.

How the picture gets dirtied, in the right order

In nature these things happen in a particular order. We copy that order instead of just adding noise at the end.

OrderWhat happensRule it comes from
1Fog, rain or haze absorbs light and washes out contrast and brightnessAtmospheric disturbance: Clear, Haze, Fog, Rain, Low light
2Moving air bends the light, so the spot wanders aboutAtmospheric turbulence
3The same moving air makes the spot flicker in brightnessAtmospheric turbulence
4The spot blurs and spreads outAtmospheric turbulence
5The whole platform drifts, up to 20 pixels per frame, in a straight line or another patternPlatform motion

10

Where the marks actually are

The rules give the marking scheme. Sixty percent sits in the two benchmark rounds, where graders hand us their own files. Those rounds are won by being ready, not by being clever.

Functional verification 20% A 10 to 15 minute live demo. Marked on: all mandatory functions present, operational success, and the GUI. We rehearse a fixed script: clear run, add each disturbance live, several targets, a video file, export the report.
Benchmark 1, scenarios 30% They give us test scenarios. Marked on: running the scenario, the centroiding error log, and the automatically generated performance log. Ours load in one click and the log and report appear without being asked for.
Benchmark 2, video files 30% They give us .mp4 files at 30 fps showing the whole screen with noise and a moving beacon. We skip our own scene and use their frames. Marked on: our centre points against their reference values, plus RMSE, acquisition and re-acquisition time, lock retention and FPS.
Technical evaluation 20% Marked on: understanding of the problem, architecture and design, choice of algorithms, AI and computer vision, innovation, documentation and presentation, and questions and answers.
The one decision that matters most. Build the video file input on day one, in step M0, before writing any detector. It is a small job then. It makes the 30 percent video round a matter of pressing "open file" instead of a rewrite the night before submission.
